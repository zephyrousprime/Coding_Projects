Evaluation

Why is this information important?

Understanding road deaths in Australia is critical because road trauma is one of the leading causes of preventable death and injury. Every fatality on the road represents a life lost and a family affected. By analysing patterns in when, where, and to whom road deaths occur, policymakers, road safety organisations, and the general public can make more informed decisions about resource allocation, enforcement strategies, and personal driving behaviour. This data directly supports the Australian Government's National Road Safety Strategy, which aims to reduce deaths and serious injuries on Australian roads.

Why is road-death data useful?

The Australian Road Deaths Database provides a structured and reliable source of information about fatal incidents on Australian roads. It includes variables such as the day of the week, month, year, road user type, state, and holiday-period indicators, which allow analysts to identify trends and patterns that would not be visible from individual incident reports. This data is useful because it transforms thousands of separate tragic events into a dataset that can be examined statistically, revealing systemic patterns such as which days carry higher risk, which road user groups are most affected, and whether road safety is improving over time.

How do the charts improve understanding?

Raw data in a spreadsheet or table is difficult to interpret at a glance. Charts transform rows of numbers into visual patterns that the human brain can process quickly. For example, the bar chart in Question 1 immediately reveals that Saturday has the highest number of road deaths, a pattern that would take much longer to identify from a sorted table. The line chart in Question 3 shows yearly trends at a glance, while the sunburst chart in Question 4 breaks down road deaths by road user type in a way that makes proportions immediately visible. By presenting data visually, the website makes complex information accessible to a wider audience, including people who may not be comfortable interpreting raw statistics.

Advantages of visualising the data

Visualising the data through interactive charts offers several advantages over static text or tables. First, users can explore the data at their own pace, clicking on a day to see monthly breakdowns (Question 1) or clicking on a year to see its monthly distribution (Question 3) allows for deeper investigation without overwhelming the user. Second, visualisations make comparisons easier: the doughnut chart for Question 2 makes the difference between Christmas and Easter periods immediately apparent. Third, the colour-coded charts help users distinguish between categories quickly. Finally, interactive elements such as drilldowns encourage curiosity and engagement, which can lead to a deeper understanding of the data.

Why are interactive charts useful?

Interactive charts go beyond static images by allowing users to engage directly with the data. In this website, several interactions enhance the analysis: clicking on a day in the Question 1 chart reveals that day's monthly breakdown, providing a layer of detail that would otherwise require a separate chart. In Question 3, clicking on a year updates the monthly bar chart to show that specific year's distribution, letting users investigate whether seasonal patterns hold consistently across different years. These interactions meet the assessment requirement for analysing data interactively and also improve the user experience by reducing information overload, users see summary data first and can drill down when interested.

Limitations

There are several important limitations to acknowledge. First, the dataset records individual road deaths, not unique crashes, a single crash with multiple fatalities generates multiple records. This means that counting records is not the same as counting crashes. Second, the dataset does not include information about traffic volume, kilometres travelled, or road conditions, so it is not possible to calculate true risk rates per exposure. For example, we cannot determine whether motorcycle riders are disproportionately risky per trip, only that they account for a significant share of deaths. Third, the holiday-period comparisons (Christmas vs Easter) use different time windows (12 days vs 5 days), so raw totals are not directly comparable without adjustment. Finally, the data does not capture non-fatal serious injuries, which represent a large portion of road trauma but are not included in this database.

How could the website be improved?

Several improvements could be made with more time. The website could incorporate traffic-volume data from state transport agencies to calculate risk-per-kilometre metrics, which would allow fairer comparisons between road user types. Additional visualisations could explore geographic patterns at a finer level (e.g., urban vs rural), time-of-day analysis, and the impact of specific road conditions. The AI analysis feature could be enhanced to provide more personalised insights based on user queries. From a technical perspective, the website could benefit from a backend server to serve processed data rather than loading the full 35MB dataset, lazy-loading of chart libraries, and improved mobile responsiveness. The evaluation section itself could be expanded with more detailed statistical analysis and comparison with international road safety data.

Data source

Australian Road Deaths Database — Australian Government

Data sourced from the Australian Road Deaths Database published by the Australian Government. The dataset contains information about road deaths in Australia, including year, month, day of week, road user type, and holiday-period indicators.

The official dictionary confirms that the Christmas Period represents the 12 days beginning 23 December, while the Easter Period represents the 5 days beginning on the Thursday before Good Friday.
